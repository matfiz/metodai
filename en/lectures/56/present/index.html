Lecture 56

Choosing and checking a mathematical method

We use uniqueness to equate the two heat kernels, choose a representation by the error it can certify on the time scale of interest, and apply both kernels to a switched source on the rod.

By the end of this lecture you will be able to:

  • Prove kernel equality by evolving common initial data and identifying the kernels.
  • Bound the two truncation errors and choose a sum for a given elapsed time.
  • Solve the switched-source problem with both kernels and verify classical regularity after the switch.
  • Check units, causality, data and limits, and distinguish a proved bound from a numerical check.

This last lecture tests the course’s methods on one rod with both ends at zero. The stages are the two kernels and their equality (Sections 56.1–56.2), truncations and a switch (Section 56.3), and the switched source with its regimes and checks (Sections 56.4–56.5). This lecture uses Lecture 37’s interval heat flow and Lecture 43’s free kernel, images and Gaussian source. As in Lectures 53–55, an exact identity and a finite approximation require different proofs.

Summary

The same zero-end interval kernel can be constructed with negative images or with decaying modes. Each construction suggests a different finite sum, and the bound on the omitted terms determines its useful time range.

  • Kernel equality. Evolve common compatible data, apply uniqueness, then use the kernel difference itself as initial data to identify the two kernels.

  • Uniform truncation bounds. Three Gaussians through Dh/L2=1/25Dh/L^2=1/25Dh/L2=1/25 and three modes thereafter keep the error below 0.0055/L0.0055/L0.0055/L on [0,L]2[0,L]^2[0,L]2.

  • Switched source. Subtract a homogeneous evolution from the static response to obtain zero initial values and classical regularity after the switch.

  • Flux and regimes. The steady outgoing flux equals the cropped input. The whole-line field has a controlled short-time error in the interior, and the slowest mode controls relaxation to equilibrium.

Choosing a method requires both a proof that its exact representation solves the problem and an estimate for the approximation used.
