Lecture 54

Regular perturbation of differential equations

We expand an equation and its initial data, then estimate the error of the resulting approximation. The oscillator shows why a small residual and accuracy on a fixed interval do not by themselves imply long-time accuracy.

By the end of this lecture you will be able to:

  • Expand an initial-value problem and its data and solve the successive linear problems.
  • Identify an unbounded correction and the resonant forcing that produces it.
  • Bound solution error using the residual, initial discrepancy and a Lipschitz constant on a finite time interval.
  • Distinguish fixed-interval accuracy from uniform accuracy on an expanding interval.

Lecture 53 expanded roots in a small parameter. Here we expand the solution of an initial-value problem, whose corrections can grow with time. The stages are a nonlinear oscillator and its energy bound, successive linear initial-value problems, a residual-to-error theorem, and a test of the approximation’s time range.

Summary

Coefficient matching constructs successive linear initial-value problems. Their unique solvability does not control the size of their solutions. For Duffing motion, resonant forcing produces secular terms even though energy bounds the exact displacement.

  • Expansion and data: the same oscillator operator occurs at each order, and each order has its own prescribed initial data.

  • Residual and error: on a finite interval [0,t1][0,t_1][0,t1​], a bounded kernel and a Lipschitz constant LLL valid on a range containing the solution and the approximation convert accumulated residual and initial discrepancy into an error bound amplified by eεLt\ee^{\varepsilon Lt}eεLt.

  • Proved range: the naive Duffing expansion has uniform error O ⁣(ε2)\bigO{\varepsilon^2}O(ε2) as ε→0+\varepsilon\to0^+ε→0+ on every fixed interval, with the explicit bound (54.19) on its stated range.

  • Limitation: a small residual for all time does not certify small error for all time. The naive error fails to tend uniformly to zero on [0,c/ε][0,c/\varepsilon][0,c/ε] when c>16/3c>16/3c>16/3.

Lecture 55 removes the secular growth by allowing the oscillator frequency to depend on ε\varepsilonε.
