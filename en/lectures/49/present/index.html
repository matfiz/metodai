Lecture 49

Particle action, Lagrangians and Hamiltonians

We derive particle equations from a stationary action and prove their equivalence to Hamilton’s equations under local Legendre inversion. We distinguish a conserved Hamiltonian from mechanical energy and recover endpoint momentum and Hamiltonian from a smoothly selected action.

By the end of this lecture you will be able to:

  • Derive Lagrange’s equations with configurations fixed at two times.
  • State the local inversion hypothesis and obtain Hamilton’s equations.
  • Distinguish time independence from equality with mechanical energy.
  • Compute endpoint derivatives for a smooth family of extremals.

Lecture 47’s several functions can describe a particle’s coordinates in time. Their variational equations become equations of motion. The stages are the action and the change from velocities to momenta (Sections 49.1–49.3), conservation and a central field (Sections 49.4–49.5), then final endpoint derivatives of the action (Section 49.6).

49.4 Time independence, conservation and energy

A quantity is conserved along a motion if its value is constant in time. A function has no explicit time dependence if its time partial derivative is zero. This differs from having zero total derivative along a particular motion.

Summary

A stationary particle action gives Lagrange’s equations. A regular velocity Hessian permits local inversion to momenta, and the resulting Hamiltonian gives equivalent first-order equations. Their solutions also determine the endpoint derivatives of a smoothly selected action.

  • Action: Hamilton’s principle requires stationarity under variations fixing the configurations at two times. It guarantees neither a minimum nor an initial velocity.

  • Legendre inversion: a nonsingular velocity Hessian gives a local inverse. Global injectivity needs further hypotheses.

  • Conservation and energy: no explicit time dependence conserves H\HamH. Homogeneous quadratic kinetic energy gives H=E\Ham=EH=E.

  • Endpoint action: a given smooth selection gives ∂qjS∗=pj\partial_{q_j}S_*=p_j∂qj​​S∗​=pj​ and ∂tS∗=−H\partial_tS_*=-\Ham∂t​S∗​=−H.

Lecture 50 replaces finitely many coordinates by the displacement of a string and separates spatial boundary conditions from temporal endpoint variations.
