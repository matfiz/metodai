Selecting a first-order ODE method

Lecture objectives

We describe solutions of first-order equations, explicit or implicit, with their interval as part of the answer. We use the existence and uniqueness theorem to find maximal solutions and exceptional solutions. We reduce homogeneous and Bernoulli equations to separable and linear ones, and record the solutions that each substitution loses.

Lectures 2 and 3 reduced Laplace’s equation for a radial field to an equation for one function of the radius, such as (r2f′)′=0,(r^2f')'=0(r2f′)′=0, and solved it by integrating twice. An equation that relates an unknown function of one variable to its derivatives is an ordinary differential equation. Equations of motion, rate laws and field equations reduced to one variable are of this kind; Lectures 4–14 develop methods for solving them. GM 33 defined an initial-value problem, solved separable and linear first-order equations, and cited a local existence and uniqueness theorem. In this lecture we find the interval on which each solution is defined, the solutions that a method loses when it divides, and substitutions that turn new equations into ones already solved.

4.1 First-order equations and their solutions

In this lecture the independent variable is xxx and the unknown function is y.yy. A prime denotes the derivative in x.xx. When the independent variable is time t,tt, a dot denotes the time derivative.

Definition 4.1 (Explicit first-order equation and its solutions). Let D⊂R2D\subset\R^2D⊂R2 be open and let F:D→RF:D\to\RF:D→R be continuous. A solution is automatically C1,C^1C1, because its derivative F(x,ϕ(x))F(x,\phi(x))F(x,ϕ(x)) is a composition of continuous functions. The graph of a solution must stay in D.DD. The interval is part of the solution: the same formula restricted to a smaller interval is a different solution.

An equation G(x,y,y′)=0G(x,y,y')=0G(x,y,y′)=0 that is not solved for the derivative is a first-order equation in implicit form. Its solutions are differentiable functions that satisfy it at every point of an open interval. Section 4.6 treats one such equation. The order of an equation is the order of the highest derivative that occurs in it, as in GM 33.

Many methods end with a relation Φ(x,y)=C\Phi(x,y)=CΦ(x,y)=C instead of a formula for y.yy. The next proposition says when such a relation describes solutions.

A general solution on a region D0⊂DD_0\subset DD0​⊂D is a family of solutions y=ϕ(x;C),y=\phi(x;C)y=ϕ(x;C), labelled by a constant C,CC, such that every initial-value problem with (x0,y0)∈D0(x_0,y_0)\in D_0(x0​,y0​)∈D0​ is solved by a member of the family. A general solution is therefore always stated together with its region.

4.2 Existence, uniqueness and maximal solutions

The local theorem of GM 33, §2 assumed a Lipschitz bound on a rectangle. On a general open set we ask for such a bound near each point.

4.7 Choosing a method

The examples follow one procedure. Lecture 5 adds exact equations and integrating factors to these methods.

  1. Write the equation as y′=F(x,y)y'=F(x,y)y′=F(x,y) where possible. Record the open set DDD where FFF is continuous and the part where ∂yF\partial_yF∂y​F is continuous. Uniqueness holds on that part.

  2. List the solutions that a division will remove: the zeros of BBB for a separable equation, the lines y=u∗xy=u_*xy=u∗​x for a homogeneous equation, and y=0y=0y=0 for a Bernoulli equation with n>0.n>0n>0.

  3. Test the form: separable A(x)B(y),A(x)B(y)A(x)B(y), linear, scaling-invariant F(λx,λy)=F(x,y),F(\lambda x,\lambda y)=F(x,y)F(λx,λy)=F(x,y), or Bernoulli. Transform the equation accordingly.

  4. Solve on the intervals where every division is valid. Solve for yyy if possible, and determine the maximal interval containing x0x_0x0​ from the formula and Theorem 4.6.

  5. Verify the equation and the initial value by substitution. Where FFF is not locally Lipschitz, look for further solutions that join an equilibrium or an envelope.

An equation can belong to several classes, as in Example 4.15. The methods then describe the same curves away from their lost solutions, and they lose different ones.

Summary

A solution of a first-order equation is a function on an interval, with graph in the domain of the equation. A first integral turns a relation Φ=C\Phi=CΦ=C into solutions. Under continuity and a local Lipschitz condition each initial-value problem has a unique maximal solution. At a finite endpoint of its interval the graph leaves every compact subset of the domain. For the whole plane the solution blows up.

Separable, homogeneous and Bernoulli equations are solved by division and substitution. Each division can lose equilibria or straight-line solutions, and uniqueness decides whether the list of lost solutions is complete. Without the Lipschitz condition a solution can reach an equilibrium, and an envelope of a family can be a singular solution.

  • Maximal solution: unique when FFF is continuous and locally Lipschitz in y.yy.

  • Finite endpoint: the graph leaves every compact subset of D.DD.

  • Homogeneous equation: y′=f(y/x);y'=f(y/x)y′=f(y/x); y=xuy=xuy=xu gives xu′=f(u)−u.xu'=f(u)-uxu′=f(u)−u.

  • Bernoulli equation: v=y1−nv=y^{1-n}v=y1−n gives a linear equation; y=0y=0y=0 is lost for n>0.n>0n>0.

  • Singular solution: uniqueness fails at each of its points; an envelope is a candidate.
