Lecture 11

Fundamental matrices and the matrix exponential

We construct fundamental matrices and the propagator of a homogeneous linear system and derive their properties from uniqueness. We define the matrix exponential by its series, prove that it is the propagator of a system with constant coefficients, and compute it from real and complex eigenpairs.

By the end of this lecture you will be able to:

  • Construct the propagator from a fundamental matrix and impose initial data with it.
  • Prove that the exponential series converges and solves the matrix equation X˙=AX\dot X=AXX˙=AX.
  • Compute etA\ee^{tA}etA for a diagonalizable matrix, and for a real matrix obtain two independent real solutions from a complex eigenpair.
  • Explain why the exponential of ∫A\int A∫A is not the propagator of a general time-dependent system.

Lecture 10 solved systems of two equations by elimination. For more components elimination becomes unwieldy, and it hides the structure of the solution. In this lecture we collect nnn independent solutions of a homogeneous linear system into a matrix. From it we build the propagator, the matrix that carries the state at one time to the state at another. For a constant coefficient matrix the propagator is the matrix exponential, defined by a power series and computed from eigenvectors.

11.1 Fundamental matrices

As in Lecture 10, JJJ is an open interval of times, and III denotes the n×nn\times nn×n identity matrix. Let A(t)A(t)A(t) be an n×nn\times nn×n matrix whose entries are continuous on JJJ. Eigenvalues of real matrices can be complex, so we allow complex entries in AAA, in the solutions and in the data. Write A=Ar+iAiA=A_{\mathrm r}+\iu A_{\mathrm i}A=Ar​+iAi​ and x=u+iw\vv x=\vv u+\iu\vv wx=u+iw with real ArA_{\mathrm r}Ar​, AiA_{\mathrm i}Ai​, u\vv uu and w\vv ww. 11.2 The propagator

A fundamental matrix is not unique, but the combination X(t)X(t0)−1X(t)X(t_0)^{-1}X(t)X(t0​)−1 in the solution formula is.

Definition 11.3 (Propagator). Let XXX be a fundamental matrix of (11.1) on JJJ. The propagator is Φ(t,τ)=X(t)X(τ)−1,t,τ∈J.\Phi(t,\tau)=X(t)X(\tau)^{-1},\qquad t,\tau\in J.Φ(t,τ)=X(t)X(τ)−1,t,τ∈J.(11.4)

In Lectures 4, 5 and 10 the letter Φ\PhiΦ denoted a first integral or a potential; from now on it denotes the propagator. A fundamental matrix exists. Its columns can be taken to be the solutions whose values at a fixed time are the unit vectors.

Proposition 11.4 (Properties of the propagator). Let t,τ,r∈Jt,\tau,r\in Jt,τ,r∈J.

  • The propagator does not depend on the choice of the fundamental matrix. For fixed τ\tauτ, the function t↦Φ(t,τ)t\mapsto\Phi(t,\tau)t↦Φ(t,τ) is the unique matrix solution with Φ(τ,τ)=I\Phi(\tau,\tau)=IΦ(τ,τ)=I.

  • For t0∈Jt_0\in Jt0​∈J and x0∈Cn\vv x_0\in\C^nx0​∈Cn, the solution of (11.1) with x(t0)=x0\vv x(t_0)=\vv x_0x(t0​)=x0​ is x(t)=Φ(t,t0)x0\vv x(t)=\Phi(t,t_0)\vv x_0x(t)=Φ(t,t0​)x0​.

  • The propagator satisfies Φ(t,τ)Φ(τ,r)=Φ(t,r)\Phi(t,\tau)\Phi(\tau,r)=\Phi(t,r)Φ(t,τ)Φ(τ,r)=Φ(t,r) and Φ(t,τ)−1=Φ(τ,t)\Phi(t,\tau)^{-1}=\Phi(\tau,t)Φ(t,τ)−1=Φ(τ,t).

Proof. First, another fundamental matrix has the form XCXCXC with CCC invertible. It gives X(t)CC−1X(τ)−1=X(t)X(τ)−1X(t)CC^{-1}X(\tau)^{-1}=X(t)X(\tau)^{-1}X(t)CC−1X(τ)−1=X(t)X(τ)−1. For fixed τ\tauτ, the function X(t)X(τ)−1X(t)X(\tau)^{-1}X(t)X(τ)−1 is a matrix solution, because the constant factor X(τ)−1X(\tau)^{-1}X(τ)−1 stands on the right. Its value at t=τt=\taut=τ is III. Any matrix solution with the value III at τ\tauτ agrees with it by uniqueness, applied to each column.

Next, the solution formula is the solution formula of Proposition 11.2.

Finally, X(t)X(τ)−1X(τ)X(r)−1=X(t)X(r)−1X(t)X(\tau)^{-1}X(\tau)X(r)^{-1}=X(t)X(r)^{-1}X(t)X(τ)−1X(τ)X(r)−1=X(t)X(r)−1. With r=tr=tr=t this gives Φ(t,τ)Φ(τ,t)=Φ(t,t)=I.\Phi(t,\tau)\Phi(\tau,t)=\Phi(t,t)=I.Φ(t,τ)Φ(τ,t)=Φ(t,t)=I.■

The propagator maps the state at the time τ\tauτ to the state at the time ttt. Column jjj of Φ(t,τ)\Phi(t,\tau)Φ(t,τ) is the solution that starts at the time τ\tauτ from the jjj-th unit vector. The composition rule says that evolving from rrr to τ\tauτ and then from τ\tauτ to ttt is the same as evolving from rrr to ttt. Lecture 13 uses the propagator to solve the inhomogeneous system.

11.4 Diagonalizable matrices

For a constant matrix the exponential is computed from eigenvectors. We recall the facts needed from LA 5 and LA 6.

A number λ∈C\lambda\in\Cλ∈C is an eigenvalue of AAA if Av=λvA\vv v=\lambda\vv vAv=λv for some nonzero v∈Cn\vv v\in\C^nv∈Cn. Such a vector is an eigenvector. The eigenvalues are the roots of the characteristic polynomial χA(λ)=det⁡(λI−A)\chi_A(\lambda)=\det(\lambda I-A)χA​(λ)=det(λI−A), which has degree nnn. Eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are linearly independent (LA 5, Theorem “Independence of eigenvectors”). 11.5 Complex eigenvalues of a real matrix

Summary

The solutions of a homogeneous linear system form an nnn-dimensional space, and a fundamental matrix has a basis of it as columns. By uniqueness a fundamental matrix is invertible at every time. The propagator Φ(t,τ)=X(t)X(τ)−1\Phi(t,\tau)=X(t)X(\tau)^{-1}Φ(t,τ)=X(t)X(τ)−1 does not depend on the choice of XXX. It carries the state at the time τ\tauτ to the state at the time ttt, and Φ(t,τ)Φ(τ,r)=Φ(t,r)\Phi(t,\tau)\Phi(\tau,r)=\Phi(t,r)Φ(t,τ)Φ(τ,r)=Φ(t,r). For a constant matrix the exponential series converges for every ttt, and e(t−τ)A\ee^{(t-\tau)A}e(t−τ)A is the propagator. For a time-dependent matrix the exponential of ∫A\int A∫A is not the propagator in general. When AAA has a basis of eigenvectors, the exponential is PetDP−1P\ee^{tD}P^{-1}PetDP−1, and every solution is a sum of modes. A complex eigenpair of a real matrix gives two linearly independent real solutions.
