Lecture 11

Fundamental matrices and the matrix exponential

Lecture objectives

We construct fundamental matrices and the propagator of a homogeneous linear system and derive their properties from uniqueness. We define the matrix exponential by its series, prove that it is the propagator of a system with constant coefficients, and compute it from real and complex eigenpairs.

Lecture 10 solved systems of two equations by elimination. For more components elimination becomes unwieldy, and it hides the structure of the solution. In this lecture we collect nnn independent solutions of a homogeneous linear system into a matrix. From it we build the propagator, the matrix that carries the state at one time to the state at another. For a constant coefficient matrix the propagator is the matrix exponential, defined by a power series and computed from eigenvectors.

11.1 Fundamental matrices

As in Lecture 10, JJJ is an open interval of times, and III denotes the n×nn\times nn×n identity matrix. Let A(t)A(t)A(t) be an n×nn\times nn×n matrix whose entries are continuous on J.JJ. Eigenvalues of real matrices can be complex, so we allow complex entries in A,AA, in the solutions and in the data. Write A=Ar+iAiA=A_{\mathrm r}+\iu A_{\mathrm i}A=Ar​+iAi​ and x=u+iw\vv x=\vv u+\iu\vv wx=u+iw with real Ar,A_{\mathrm r}Ar​, Ai,A_{\mathrm i}Ai​, u\vv uu and w.\vv ww. The complex system x˙=Ax\dot{\vv x}=A\vv xx˙=Ax is then equivalent to the real system u˙=Aru−Aiw,w˙=Aiu+Arw\dot{\vv u}=A_{\mathrm r}\vv u-A_{\mathrm i}\vv w,\qquad \dot{\vv w}=A_{\mathrm i}\vv u+A_{\mathrm r}\vv wu˙=Ar​u−Ai​w,w˙=Ai​u+Ar​w for 2n2n2n real unknowns. The existence and uniqueness theorem of Lecture 7 applies to it. Hence each initial-value problem with complex data has exactly one solution on J,JJ, and the counting theorem of Lecture 10 holds with Cn\C^nCn in place of Rn,\R^nRn, with the same proof. Let SSS be the complex space of solutions on JJJ of the homogeneous system x˙=A(t)x.\dot{\vv x}=A(t)\vv x.x˙=A(t)x.(11.1) Then dim⁡S=n,\dim S=ndimS=n, and x↦x(t0)\vv x\mapsto\vv x(t_0)x↦x(t0​) is a linear bijection from SSS to Cn\C^nCn for every t0∈J.t_0\in Jt0​∈J.

Derivatives and integrals of matrix functions are taken entrywise. The product rule  ⁣d ⁣dt(XY)=X˙Y+XY˙\frac{\dd}{\dd t}(XY)=\dot XY+X\dot Ydtd​(XY)=X˙Y+XY˙ holds, with the order of the factors kept, because each entry of XYXYXY is a sum of products of entries.

Definition 11.1 (Matrix solution and fundamental matrix). A matrix solution of (11.1) on JJJ is a C1C^1C1 function XXX from JJJ to the n×nn\times nn×n matrices with X˙=A(t)Xon J.\dot X=A(t)X\qquad\text{on }J.X˙=A(t)Xon J.(11.2) It is a fundamental matrix if its columns are linearly independent elements of S.SS.

Column jjj of AXA XAX is AAA times column jjj of X.XX. Hence XXX is a matrix solution exactly when each of its columns solves (11.1). A fundamental matrix has a basis of SSS as its columns.

Proposition 11.2 (Fundamental matrices). Let XXX be a matrix solution of (11.1) on J.JJ.

  • For every constant vector c∈Cn\vv c\in\C^nc∈Cn the function X(t)cX(t)\vv cX(t)c solves (11.1).

  • The matrix XXX is a fundamental matrix if and only if det⁡X(t0)≠0\det X(t_0)\ne0detX(t0​)=0 at one point t0∈J,t_0\in Jt0​∈J, and then det⁡X(t)≠0\det X(t)\ne0detX(t)=0 at every t∈J.t\in Jt∈J.

  • If XXX is a fundamental matrix, t0∈Jt_0\in Jt0​∈J and x0∈Cn,\vv x_0\in\C^nx0​∈Cn, the solution with x(t0)=x0\vv x(t_0)=\vv x_0x(t0​)=x0​ is x(t)=X(t)X(t0)−1x0.\vv x(t)=X(t)X(t_0)^{-1}\vv x_0x(t)=X(t)X(t0​)−1x0​.

Proof. First, X(t)c=∑jcj (column j)X(t)\vv c=\sum_jc_j\,(\text{column }j)X(t)c=∑j​cj​(column j) is a linear combination of solutions.

Next, the map x↦x(t0)\vv x\mapsto\vv x(t_0)x↦x(t0​) from SSS to Cn\C^nCn is a linear bijection. A linear bijection maps linearly independent vectors to linearly independent vectors, and so does its inverse. Hence the columns of XXX are independent exactly when their values at t0,t_0t0​, the columns of X(t0),X(t_0)X(t0​), are independent. For a square matrix this is equivalent to det⁡X(t0)≠0\det X(t_0)\ne0detX(t0​)=0 (GM 22). Then, X(t)X(t0)−1x0X(t)X(t_0)^{-1}\vv x_0X(t)X(t0​)−1x0​ solves (11.1), being of the form X(t)c,X(t)\vv cX(t)c, and its value at t0t_0t0​ is x0.\vv x_0x0​. By uniqueness it is the solution.

11.2 The propagator

A fundamental matrix is not unique, but the combination X(t)X(t0)−1X(t)X(t_0)^{-1}X(t)X(t0​)−1 in the solution formula is.

Definition 11.3 (Propagator). Let XXX be a fundamental matrix of (11.1) on J.JJ. The propagator is Φ(t,τ)=X(t)X(τ)−1,t,τ∈J.\Phi(t,\tau)=X(t)X(\tau)^{-1},\qquad t,\tau\in J.Φ(t,τ)=X(t)X(τ)−1,t,τ∈J.(11.4)

In Lectures 4, 5 and 10 the letter Φ\PhiΦ denoted a first integral or a potential; from now on it denotes the propagator. A fundamental matrix exists. Its columns can be taken to be the solutions whose values at a fixed time are the unit vectors.

Proposition 11.4 (Properties of the propagator). Let t,τ,r∈J.t,\tau,r\in Jt,τ,r∈J.

  • The propagator does not depend on the choice of the fundamental matrix. For fixed τ,\tauτ, the function t↦Φ(t,τ)t\mapsto\Phi(t,\tau)t↦Φ(t,τ) is the unique matrix solution with Φ(τ,τ)=I.\Phi(\tau,\tau)=IΦ(τ,τ)=I.

  • For t0∈Jt_0\in Jt0​∈J and x0∈Cn,\vv x_0\in\C^nx0​∈Cn, the solution of (11.1) with x(t0)=x0\vv x(t_0)=\vv x_0x(t0​)=x0​ is x(t)=Φ(t,t0)x0.\vv x(t)=\Phi(t,t_0)\vv x_0x(t)=Φ(t,t0​)x0​.

  • The propagator satisfies Φ(t,τ)Φ(τ,r)=Φ(t,r)\Phi(t,\tau)\Phi(\tau,r)=\Phi(t,r)Φ(t,τ)Φ(τ,r)=Φ(t,r) and Φ(t,τ)−1=Φ(τ,t).\Phi(t,\tau)^{-1}=\Phi(\tau,t)Φ(t,τ)−1=Φ(τ,t).

Proof. First, another fundamental matrix has the form XCXCXC with CCC invertible. It gives X(t)CC−1X(τ)−1=X(t)X(τ)−1.X(t)CC^{-1}X(\tau)^{-1}=X(t)X(\tau)^{-1}X(t)CC−1X(τ)−1=X(t)X(τ)−1. For fixed τ,\tauτ, the function X(t)X(τ)−1X(t)X(\tau)^{-1}X(t)X(τ)−1 is a matrix solution, because the constant factor X(τ)−1X(\tau)^{-1}X(τ)−1 stands on the right. Its value at t=τt=\taut=τ is I.II. Any matrix solution with the value III at τ\tauτ agrees with it by uniqueness, applied to each column.

Next, the solution formula is the solution formula of Proposition 11.2.

Finally, X(t)X(τ)−1X(τ)X(r)−1=X(t)X(r)−1.X(t)X(\tau)^{-1}X(\tau)X(r)^{-1}=X(t)X(r)^{-1}X(t)X(τ)−1X(τ)X(r)−1=X(t)X(r)−1. With r=tr=tr=t this gives Φ(t,τ)Φ(τ,t)=Φ(t,t)=I.\Phi(t,\tau)\Phi(\tau,t)=\Phi(t,t)=I.Φ(t,τ)Φ(τ,t)=Φ(t,t)=I.■

The propagator maps the state at the time τ\tauτ to the state at the time t.tt. Column jjj of Φ(t,τ)\Phi(t,\tau)Φ(t,τ) is the solution that starts at the time τ\tauτ from the jjj-th unit vector. The composition rule says that evolving from rrr to τ\tauτ and then from τ\tauτ to ttt is the same as evolving from rrr to t.tt. Lecture 13 uses the propagator to solve the inhomogeneous system.

11.3 The matrix exponential

For a scalar equation x˙=ax\dot x=axx˙=ax with a constant aaa the propagator is ea(t−τ).\ee^{a(t-\tau)}ea(t−τ). For a constant matrix we define the exponential by the same power series. 11.4 Diagonalizable matrices

For a constant matrix the exponential is computed from eigenvectors. We recall the facts needed from LA 5 and LA 6.

11.5 Complex eigenvalues of a real matrix

A real matrix can have complex eigenvalues. Its exponential is real, because the series (11.5) has real terms. Real solutions are obtained from a complex eigenpair as follows.

Summary

The solutions of a homogeneous linear system form an nnn-dimensional space, and a fundamental matrix has a basis of it as columns. By uniqueness a fundamental matrix is invertible at every time. The propagator Φ(t,τ)=X(t)X(τ)−1\Phi(t,\tau)=X(t)X(\tau)^{-1}Φ(t,τ)=X(t)X(τ)−1 does not depend on the choice of X.XX. It carries the state at the time τ\tauτ to the state at the time t,tt, and Φ(t,τ)Φ(τ,r)=Φ(t,r).\Phi(t,\tau)\Phi(\tau,r)=\Phi(t,r)Φ(t,τ)Φ(τ,r)=Φ(t,r). For a constant matrix the exponential series converges for every t,tt, and e(t−τ)A\ee^{(t-\tau)A}e(t−τ)A is the propagator. When AAA has a basis of eigenvectors, the exponential is PetDP−1,P\ee^{tD}P^{-1}PetDP−1, and every solution is a sum of modes.
