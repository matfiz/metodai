Lecture 6

Reducing the order of differential equations

We reduce second-order equations without the unknown, or without the independent variable, to first-order equations, and reconstruct the solution with its two initial data. For conservative motion we derive the energy integral and show what happens at turning points, where the reduced equation has solutions that the original equation does not.

By the end of this lecture you will be able to:

  • Recognise a missing unknown or a missing independent variable in a second-order equation.
  • Perform the reduction and recover both initial conditions.
  • Derive the energy integral of a conservative motion and use it to find a time of travel.
  • Treat turning points and equilibria without dividing by zero silently.

Newton’s equation of motion is of second order: it prescribes an acceleration. Lectures 7–9 treat linear second-order equations. In this lecture we reduce nonlinear second-order equations to first-order ones, first when the unknown function itself is missing, and next when the independent variable is missing. Conservative motion in one dimension belongs to the second case, and its reduction is the energy integral.

Summary

A second-order equation needs a value and a slope as initial data. When the unknown is missing, its derivative solves a first-order equation and one integration recovers the unknown. When the independent variable is missing, the slope becomes a function of the value, y′′=P ⁣dP/ ⁣dyy''=P\dd P/\dd yy′′=PdP/dy, on intervals where y′≠0y'\ne0y′=0. Equilibria and turning points lie outside that reduction and are treated separately.

For conservative motion the reduction is the energy integral. It holds for every motion, but its first-order form also admits constant functions at points where V(q)=EV(q)=EV(q)=E and V′(q)≠0V'(q)\ne0V′(q)=0, which are not motions.
