Lecture 41

Membranes, Helmholtz equations and nodal patterns

Lecture objectives

We separate the membrane equation into the Helmholtz eigenvalue problem and an oscillator equation, prove that the eigenvalues are positive and that the eigenfunctions of different eigenvalues are orthogonal, and prove that the product modes of a rectangle and the Bessel modes of a disc span each eigenspace. We compare the frequencies and nodal lines of a square and of a disc.

Lecture 32 found the modes of a string with fixed ends, and Lecture 39 found the radial modes of a disc. A drum is a membrane stretched over a frame. Its standing waves solve the Helmholtz equation in a plane region with zero boundary values. In this lecture we find all standing waves of a rectangular and of a circular membrane, count how many independent modes share one frequency, and see how the lines at rest, the nodal lines, depend on the geometry and on the choice of mode within a repeated frequency.

Summary

Standing waves w(x)q(t)w(\vv x)q(t)w(x)q(t) of a membrane with a fixed edge lead to the Helmholtz eigenvalue problem −∇2w=λw,-\laplacian w=\lambda w−∇2w=λw, w=0w=0w=0 on the boundary. Green’s identity shows that the eigenvalues are positive and that modes of different eigenvalues are orthogonal. The modes of a rectangle are products of sines, and those of a disc are Jm(jm,nr/a)J_m(j_{m,n}r/a)Jm​(jm,n​r/a) times cos⁡(mφ)\cos(m\varphi)cos(mφ) or sin⁡(mφ).\sin(m\varphi)sin(mφ). In both cases these modes span each eigenspace, so the multiplicity of an eigenvalue is the number of independent listed modes with that eigenvalue. Within a repeated eigenvalue every nonzero combination is a mode, and the nodal lines change with the combination.
