Learning with AI, not from it

An AI assistant will explain anything at any hour — and it will just as happily do your thinking for you. Use it the first way: pick a lecture section below and get a prompt that makes it explain, question and check, then hand the work back to you.

What a good session looks like

Lecture 19: closing a contour to evaluate a real integral.

You

Why can I drop the semicircle when I compute ∫ cos x /(1 + x²) dx with residues?

AI

Before I answer: on the arc |z| = R in the upper half-plane, what bound can you give for |eⁱᶻ/(1 + z²)|? What does it give for the arc integral as R → ∞?

It asks for the estimate instead of quoting a lemma.

You

There |eⁱᶻ| ≤ 1 and |1 + z²| ≥ R² − 1, so the arc integral is at most πR/(R² − 1), which tends to 0.

AI

Good. Now: why did you integrate eⁱᶻ/(1 + z²) on the arc rather than cos z/(1 + z²)?

Then it tests the step most often skipped: cos z grows in the upper half-plane.

Three habits that make the difference

  • Name the hypothesis. Ask what a result needs — function class, domain, convergence — not only what it says.
  • Do the work. Ask for an exercise or a hint, attempt it, and only then ask for feedback.
  • Check with a special case. Test every answer, the assistant's included, on a limiting case, the units or a symmetry.

Longer sessions

Four Socratic prompts for longer work. Fill in the [bracketed fields] in the chat.

Choose a methodBefore solving a problem, decide how to attack it.
I am studying Methods of Mathematical Physics. Problem: [paste the problem]

Do not solve it. Help me choose a method. Ask me ONE question at a time and wait for my answer:
1. What kind of problem is it: an ODE, a PDE, an integral, a sum or a functional? What are the domain and the boundary or initial conditions?
2. Which methods could apply — separation of variables, a transform, characteristics, a Green's function, residues, a series, a variational or a perturbative method — and what does each require?
3. Make me compare two candidate methods and name the hypotheses each needs here.
Stop when I can say in two sentences which method I will use and why.
Audit the hypothesesWhen a calculation looks right but you are not sure it is justified.
Here is my calculation: [paste it]

Do not redo it. Go through it with me and find every step that exchanges two limiting operations or uses a hidden assumption: differentiating under an integral, integrating a series term by term, closing or deforming a contour, discarding an arc, inverting a transform, or using a condition at a singular point.
For each such step ask me ONE question at a time: which theorem justifies it, and is its hypothesis met here? If a step is not justified, ask me for a counterexample or for a stronger hypothesis that would fix it.
Check an answer without the solutionAfter an exercise, before looking at the final answer.
I have solved this problem: [paste the problem]
My answer: [paste the answer]

Do not tell me whether it is right. Help me test it myself. Ask me, one at a time, for:
1. the units or dimensions of each term;
2. a limiting or special case whose answer I know;
3. a symmetry the answer must respect;
4. substitution back into the equation and the boundary or initial conditions;
5. the behaviour for large and small values of the variables.
After each check ask me what it shows. At the end ask me how confident I am, and why.
Translate a conventionWhen a textbook uses other signs or normalisations.
My course uses the Fourier transform f̂(k) = (2π)^(−1/2) ∫ e^(−ikx) f(x) dx and the one-sided Laplace transform F(s) = ∫₀^∞ e^(−st) f(t) dt with the 0⁻ convention for impulses at t = 0. A textbook writes: [paste its formula and its convention]

Do not convert it for me. Ask me which constants and signs differ, have me convert one formula step by step, and then have me check the result on a test function whose transform I know, such as a Gaussian.

Working in pairs

  1. Pair up. Each of you sets a problem on the lecture and works your own model solution — privately.
  2. Swap. Hand over only the problem, not your solution.
  3. Solve. Each of you works the other's problem; use the AI only for hints, never for the answer.
  4. Debrief without AI. Did the solutions agree? Did the problem have a hole — a missing condition or hypothesis? Was the setter's own model answer actually right? This human step is the point of the exercise.

Two traps to avoid

The setter must verify their own model solution. Do not trust an AI-generated answer key. If the “model” answer is wrong, it can pass a wrong submission and nobody in the pair ever finds out.

The solver must resist pasting the problem in and asking for the answer. The skill being trained is choosing a method and justifying each step, not retrieval. The whole exercise depends on not doing this.
