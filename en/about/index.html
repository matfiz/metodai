About the course

A rigorous self-study course in the analytical methods of physics.

The course

Methods of Mathematical Physics develops the analytical methods used to formulate, solve and interpret physical problems. Part I develops fields and ordinary differential equations. Part II establishes complex analysis and integral transforms. Part III treats partial differential equations, boundary-value problems and eigenfunction methods. Part IV develops evolution kernels, stochastic models, variational principles and approximation methods.

Lectures and exercises

There are 56 lectures, fourteen in each part, numbered continuously and meant to be read in order. Each lecture develops one principal method: it states its objectives, derives its results with their hypotheses, works complete examples and closes with a summary and its references. A lecture is a unit of self-study, not a timed class; give it the time its arguments need.

Every lecture has a companion exercise sheet. Each exercise has a hint and its final answer, both hidden until you ask for them, so you can attempt it before seeing any help. The sheet is also available as a PDF.

Preparation

Start after General Mathematics or with equivalent competence in calculus, complex arithmetic, vector geometry and computational matrix algebra. Linear Algebra for Physicists is a companion reference throughout: when a lecture needs a result from it, the lecture names the place to read, and you do not need to complete that course first. Each lecture's references list what to revise before it.

What you will be able to do

  • select coordinates and derive the differential operators needed for a field problem;
  • classify common scalar ODEs and solve initial-value problems and homogeneous and inhomogeneous systems with explicit hypotheses;
  • use analyticity, branches, singularities and residues to evaluate integrals, sums and inverse transforms;
  • use Fourier and Laplace transforms with a stated convention, convergence conditions and the interpretation of distributions;
  • formulate initial and boundary conditions for wave, heat, diffusion and potential problems, then select characteristics, separation, transforms or Green's functions;
  • construct and normalise eigenfunction expansions, explaining the role of boundary conditions and degeneracy;
  • derive and solve elementary Fokker–Planck models, including free diffusion and the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process;
  • use complex potentials and conformal maps to solve planar Laplace boundary-value problems;
  • obtain Euler–Lagrange equations and endpoint and constraint conditions from a functional, and apply action principles to physical models;
  • construct and check asymptotic and perturbative approximations, including their useful parameter and time ranges.

Two languages

The course is written in Lithuanian and English. Both editions have the same lectures, numbering and exercises; switch with LT | EN at the top of any page.
